Research

1. Principles of Enzyme Selectivity

Enzymes are highly specific biological catalysts. This selectivity originates from their unique 3D structures. The active site of an enzyme is precisely shaped to bind with specific substrates (drugs). This interaction is traditionally explained by the 'Lock and Key' model, and more accurately by the 'Induced Fit' model, where the enzyme undergoes conformational changes to tightly bind the drug molecule.

2. The Process of Drug Metabolism

Drug metabolism primarily occurs in the liver, transforming lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs into hydrophilic (water-soluble) molecules so they can be excreted. The Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) family of enzymes plays the most critical role in this process. Different CYP enzymes (e.g., CYP3A4, CYP2D6) recognize and metabolize different structural classes of drugs.

3. Research Focus: Substrate Competition

When multiple drugs are taken simultaneously, they may compete for the same enzyme's active site. This research explores how varying substrate affinities lead to competitive inhibition, potentially causing drug toxicity or altering the therapeutic effect.

Analysis: So What Can We Learn?

Analyzing enzyme selectivity and metabolic data provides crucial insights into why the same medication affects people differently and how we can optimize treatments.

Key Finding: Genetic Polymorphism dictates Drug Efficacy
Variations in DNA sequence cause differences in enzyme activity levels among individuals.

1. Individual Differences in Drug Responses

Through data analysis, we can categorize populations into four phenotypes based on their enzyme activity: Poor Metabolizers (PM), Intermediate Metabolizers (IM), Extensive Metabolizers (EM), and Ultrarapid Metabolizers (UM). For a 'Poor Metabolizer', a standard drug dose might not be broken down quickly enough, leading to dangerous toxicity. Conversely, an 'Ultrarapid Metabolizer' might break down the drug too fast, rendering it ineffective.

2. Clinical Applications & Pharmacogenomics

By analyzing the graphs of enzyme reaction rates and understanding metabolic pathways, medical professionals can predict adverse drug reactions (ADRs). This leads to the field of Pharmacogenomics (맞춤의학), where a patient's genetic profile is used to select the safest and most effective drug and dosage.

3. Conclusion from Data

The visual and theoretical data confirms that drug metabolism is not a "one-size-fits-all" process. Understanding the selectivity and kinetic limits of enzymes like CYP450 is essential for safe pharmaceutical engineering and precision medicine.
